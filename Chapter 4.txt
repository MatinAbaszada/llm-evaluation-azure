Chapter 4. Results

This chapter reports the empirical observations of the study. All numbers, tables, and figures in this chapter follow the measurement and aggregation rules defined in Chapter 3. Accuracy, latency, cost, and economic reward are both computed per record and then macro-averaged across the five benchmark datasets, in order to ensure that each dataset contributes equally regardless of its sample count (cf. Section 3.3.1 and the reward equation in Section 3.3.4).


================================================================================
4.1 Standalone Model Results
================================================================================

The standalone evaluation establishes the reference point for all subsequent optimization analyses. Each of the six Azure-hosted models introduced in Section 3.2.1 was evaluated on every benchmark from Section 3.2.2 using the prompts and decoding settings of Section 3.2.3.  To get the answer for each question, exactly one API call was made, without any routing, escalation, or resampling.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.1.1 Accuracy and Dataset-Level Performance
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The figure below shows the macro-average score of each individual model, where each of the five datasets carries equal weight.

[chart1_accuracy.png]

Based on macro-average ranking, the models are separated into two groups. The first group consists of reasoning models, namely gpt-5.4-pro (93.2%), gpt-5.4 (90.8%), and o3-mini (83.3%), and all of them surpass the 80% threshold. On the other hand, the second group includes gpt-4.1 (68.3%), gpt-5.4-mini (60.6%), and gpt-4.1-mini (57.6%), which sit between 57% and 69%. o3-mini sets the lower bound of the top tier at 83.3%, and gpt-4.1 defines the upper bound of the second tier at 68.3%. The gap between them is roughly 15 percent, exceeding any gap within either tier.

[chart2_accuracy_per_dataset.png]

At the level of individual datasets, the macro-average rank is not consistent for the tasks. On HumanEval and MBPP all six models score between 81% and 100%. Even the weakest model in the macro ranking, gpt-4.1-mini, reaches 91.4% on HumanEval and 82.7% on MBPP. The two coding benchmarks therefore offer little discriminating power for this set of models. The picture inverts on GPQA: the spread is the widest of any dataset (34.3% for gpt-4.1-mini up to 84.0% for gpt-5.4), and o3-mini, despite being a dedicated reasoning model, reaches only 52.8%. The result of o3-mini is even below the non-reasoning gpt-5.4 (84.0%) and gpt-5.4-pro (82.0%). Regarding the MMLU-Pro, the 4.1-generation pair stays at 50.1% and 27.8%, while the 5.4-generation pair reaches 90.0% and 41.6%. It is possible to see a bimodal distribution in the GSM8K dataset because the low lewel models are clustered around 52%, whereas the high level models exceed 92%.

Between the models, gpt-4.1, is a noteworthy outlier. It achieved 92.4% average accuracy on the HumanEval tasks and 100.0% on the MBPP tasks, indicating good performance on coding tasks. However, on knowledge and reasoning benchmarks, the model scores just 50.1% on MMLU-Pro, 45.7% on GPQA, and 53.1% on GSM8K. Even though the performance is distributed unevenly, the macro average of the model hides this pattern.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.1.2 Latency Results and Observed API Effects
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Wall-clock latency was measured per request as defined in Section 3.3.2. The table below summarises both mean and median per-request latency along with the number of requests that exceeded 120 seconds and the maximum value observed during the standalone runs.

  Model         | Mean (s) | Median (s) | # outliers >120s | Max (s)
  --------------+----------+------------+------------------+---------
  gpt-4.1-mini  |   17.4   |     9.6    |       35         |  634.7
  gpt-5.4-mini  |   18.4   |     8.2    |       23         |  630.9
  gpt-4.1       |   28.1   |     6.0    |       54         |  648.9
  gpt-5.4       |   34.7   |     6.1    |       10         |  607.5
  gpt-5.4-pro   |   53.1   |    17.8    |       32         |  888.8
  o3-mini       |   65.7   |    15.4    |      107         |  760.0

Conventional patter could be observed for the mean ranking. On average, the small model is faster than its larger counterpart (gpt-4.1-mini 17.4s vs. gpt-4.1 28.1s; gpt-5.4-mini 18.4s vs. gpt-5.4 34.7s). The reverse is true for the medians, where gpt-4.1 (6.0s) and gpt-5.4 (6.1s) are below their respective minis (9.6s and 8.2s). The low median with a higher mean indicates that the bulk of large-model requests complete quickly, but a small number of long-running outliers shift the average upward. The same effect drives the means of the minis themselves. Requests above 120s are roughly similar across all deployments (10 to 54 for the four general-purpose models, 32 for gpt-5.4-pro, and 107 for o3-mini). This matches with the description written in section 2.5. As the quota tier is reached, deployment latency degrades, and this is usually the case for reasoning-enabled models because they perform additional internal processing.

A smaller, genuine effect is also present. The mini models emit substantially more completion tokens than their larger siblings on the multiple-choice and short-answer benchmarks. On MMLU-Pro, gpt-4.1 produces an average of 9 completion tokens per request, while gpt-4.1-mini produces 150.  This variation in the number of tokens leads to a minor increase in latency even in unthrottled requests. Therefore, instead of pure model speeds, the reported mean latencies should be understood as deployment-level observations.

The two reasoning-enabled models exhibit the longest tails. Average of o3-mini was 65.7s, mostly due to MMLU-Pro (104.1s per request) and the same was 53.1s for gpt-5.4-pro, mostly due to GPQA (104.9s per request). Both behaviors are consistent with the architectural property discussed in Section 2.3, which states that for complex tasks, inference generation takes longer due to internal deliberation, while the generation time remains reasonable for simpler ones, such as GSM8K (18.7s for o3-mini, 14.1s for gpt-5.4-pro).

4.1.3 Token Usage and Cost Results
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Per-request cost was computed deterministically from token counts using the cost equation of Section 3.3.3 and the Azure OpenAI list prices recorded there. The table below reports the macro-average cost per request and the cost on GPQA, which turns out to be the most expensive dataset for every model. Values are reported in milli-dollars (1 m$ = 0.001 USD).

  Model         | Macro avg cost/req (m$) | GPQA cost/req (m$)
  --------------+-------------------------+--------------------
  gpt-5.4-mini  |          0.138          |        0.124
  gpt-4.1-mini  |          0.255          |        0.346
  gpt-4.1       |          0.687          |        0.775
  o3-mini       |          4.659          |        8.805
  gpt-5.4       |          5.370          |       14.808
  gpt-5.4-pro   |        106.021          |      295.182

The difference between the lowest price at 0.138 m$ per request for the gpt-5.4-mini model and the highest price of 106.021 m$ per request for the gpt-5.4-pro model spans nearly three orders of magnitude, which represents a difference of about 769x. GPQA is consistently the most expensive dataset across all models because of its long, multi-part scientific prompts and the longer reasoning traces they elicit. The GPQA cost reaches 295.182 m$ per request for gpt-5.4-pro and 14.808 m$ for gpt-5.4. Within the second tier, even though gpt-5.4-mini is a newer generation, it was even cheaper than gpt-4.1-mini.

[chart3_cost_vs_accuracy.png]

The cost-accuracy plot allows to observe those models lying on the empirical Pareto frontier. gpt-5.4-mini occupies the cheapest corner while gpt-5.4 dominates the upper-middle of the plane by combining 90.8% accuracy with a cost roughly 20x below gpt-5.4-pro. gpt-5.4-pro achieves the maximum accuracy, but because of a cost constraint, it is placed far to the right of every other point. o3-mini and gpt-4.1 are not Pareto-dominant under this projection. Both of them are matched or exceeded by another model in either accuracy or cost.

================================================================================
4.2 Cascade Strategy Results
================================================================================

The cascade strategy specified in Section 3.4.3 first routes each input through a small model, then forwards it to the large model if its confidence falls below the threshold T. A total of twelve configurations were evaluated. These include four small and large pairings combined with three thresholds T in {60, 75, 90}.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.2.1 Escalation Behavior and Accuracy
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The single most important measurement of the cascade experiment is the escalation rate, since the value of the strategy depends entirely on how often the large model is actually invoked. The table below reports the macro-average escalation rate for each configuration.

  Configuration                  | T=60 | T=75 | T=90
  -------------------------------+------+------+------
  gpt-4.1-mini -> gpt-4.1        | 0.1% | 0.4% | 1.1%
  gpt-4.1-mini -> gpt-5.4        | 0.0% | 0.1% | 0.1%
  gpt-5.4-mini -> gpt-4.1        | 0.1% | 1.7% | 7.3%
  gpt-5.4-mini -> gpt-5.4        | 0.0% | 0.2% | 1.0%

Even at the most aggressive threshold, where T was set to 90, there was only 7.3% escalation with the median escalation rate of the twelve experiments falling below 1%. At T=60 and T=75, there was almost no escalation for most pairings. So, in most cases, the small model produced the final answer alone. Figure 3.4.2 plots the escalation rate and the achieved per dataset accuracy for each configuration, and it is obvious that the same pattern holds at the dataset level.

[chartC1_cascade_combined_overview.png]

The mechanical consequence is that the threshold parameter T has almost no effect on cascade accuracy. In all variants, the macro-average accuracy varies by at most 0.5 pp across all three thresholds. The table below reports the values, along with the small and large standalone baselines, for reference.

  Configuration                  | T=60  | T=75  | T=90  | Small alone | Large alone
  -------------------------------+-------+-------+-------+-------------+-------------
  gpt-4.1-mini -> gpt-4.1        | 57.6% | 57.7% | 57.7% |    57.6%    |    68.3%
  gpt-4.1-mini -> gpt-5.4        | 57.6% | 57.6% | 57.6% |    57.6%    |    90.8%
  gpt-5.4-mini -> gpt-4.1        | 60.6% | 60.4% | 60.5% |    60.6%    |    68.3%
  gpt-5.4-mini -> gpt-5.4        | 60.6% | 60.7% | 61.1% |    60.6%    |    90.8%

The largest visible increase is 0.5 pp, which can be seen when increasing the temperature to 90 for the gpt-5.4-mini -> gpt-5.4 cell. This improvement is the result of increases in GPQA (39.6% to 40.7%) and MMLU-Pro (41.6% to 42.5%). This aligns with the observation that the model tends to return lower confidence on harder questions. In contrast, for the gpt-5.4-mini -> gpt-4.1 cell, the questions escalated by temperature increase go to a model that only has a slightly higher GPQA accuracy than the gpt-5.4-mini (45.7% vs. 39.6%), so there is not a noticeable macro-accuracy improvement even though there was a slight accuracy gain in the cell for the escalated subset.

The reason for such a low escalation rate is that the modern small models are excessively confident and rarely produce confidence values below 0.6, 0.75, or even 0.9. Therefore, the escalation gate almost never opens at any of the three thresholds tested, regardless of whether the underlying answer is correct or wrong. This forces the cascade pipeline to run the small model alone for the overwhelming majority of inputs.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.2.2 Latency and Cost Trade-Offs
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Because escalation is rare, the latency of every cascade configuration is dominated by the latency of its small model. The macro-average latency of the gpt-4.1-mini-based cascades stays around 17.7–18.3s and that of the gpt-5.4-mini-based cascades around 28.5–31.3s. They are slightly higher than the standalone metrics shown in Section 4.1.2 (17.4s and 18.4s, respectively) due to a small number of escalated requests that took longer to execute. The large-model component contributes only the additional time required to handle the few escalated requests.

The cost picture is qualitatively similar but not identical. The table below compares the macro-average cost per request between T=60 and T=90 for each pairing.

  Configuration                  | T=60 cost (m$) | T=90 cost (m$) | Small alone (m$)
  -------------------------------+----------------+----------------+------------------
  gpt-4.1-mini -> gpt-4.1        |     0.255      |     0.262      |       0.255
  gpt-4.1-mini -> gpt-5.4        |     0.255      |     0.275      |       0.255
  gpt-5.4-mini -> gpt-4.1        |     0.139      |     0.199      |       0.138
  gpt-5.4-mini -> gpt-5.4        |     0.141      |     0.311      |       0.138

At T=60 the cost is essentially that of the small model. However, at T=90, the gpt-5.4-mini -> gpt-5.4 pairing rises from 0.141 m$ to 0.311 m$, meaning a 121% increase due solely to the 1.0% of inputs routed to the expensive large model in the case of hard questions. The same pairing on GPQA alone moves from 0.124 m$ at T=60 to 0.748 m$ at T=90, a sixfold increase caused by a 3.0% escalation rate. The cost asymmetry documented in Section 4.1.3 reflects this effect. A single GPQA call to gpt-5.4 costs around 14.8 m$, which leads to the macro-average being dominated by a small number of escalations.

================================================================================
4.3 Router Strategy Results
================================================================================

The router strategy specified in Section 3.4.2 replaces the post-hoc confidence gate of the cascade with an upfront classifier model that, for each input, decides whether the small or the large model should produce the answer. Four configurations were evaluated, crossing two router models (gpt-4.1-mini, gpt-5.4-mini) with two small/large pairings.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.3.1 Routing Behavior and Accuracy
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

In contrast to the cascade, the router actually directs a substantial fraction of inputs to the large model. The table below reports the per-dataset routing rate to the large model for every configuration.

  Configuration                       | Overall | HumanEval | MBPP  | MMLU-Pro | GPQA  | GSM8K
  ------------------------------------+---------+-----------+-------+----------+-------+-------
  rtr=4.1-mini, 4.1-mini -> 4.1       |  11.4%  |   1.8%    | 1.4%  |  16.0%   | 40.8% |  0.0%
  rtr=4.1-mini, 5.4-mini -> 5.4       |  10.7%  |   1.2%    | 1.1%  |  15.3%   | 39.8% |  0.0%
  rtr=5.4-mini, 4.1-mini -> 4.1       |  25.1%  |   0.6%    | 1.6%  |  36.8%   | 42.2% | 21.4%
  rtr=5.4-mini, 5.4-mini -> 5.4       |  26.3%  |   0.6%    | 0.6%  |  36.6%   | 43.3% | 25.8%

The routing pattern is task-aware. Because both code benchmarks are already handled well by the small model, all four configurations send less than 2% of HumanEval and MBPP inputs to the large model, as Section 4.1.1 showed. The bulk of the escalation is concentrated on GPQA, at around 40–43% across all configurations, and on MMLU-Pro, at around 16–37%. There was a sharp difference in how routers treated GSM8K. The gpt-4.1-mini router treats GSM8K as easy and does not escalate any question, while the gpt-5.4-mini router escalates 21–26% of GSM8K inputs. The figure below visualizes the routing split and the per-dataset accuracy for each configuration.

[chartR1_router_overview.png]

The accuracy outcomes follow directly from the routing behavior. The table below reports the macro-average accuracy of every configuration alongside the gain over the corresponding small-model baseline.

  Configuration                       | Router acc | Small alone | Large alone | Delta vs small
  ------------------------------------+------------+-------------+-------------+----------------
  rtr=4.1-mini, 4.1-mini -> 4.1       |   58.2%    |    57.6%    |    68.3%    |    +0.6 pp
  rtr=4.1-mini, 5.4-mini -> 5.4       |   60.8%    |    60.6%    |    90.8%    |    +0.2 pp
  rtr=5.4-mini, 4.1-mini -> 4.1       |   73.1%    |    57.6%    |    68.3%    |   +15.5 pp
  rtr=5.4-mini, 5.4-mini -> 5.4       |   77.0%    |    60.6%    |    90.8%    |   +16.4 pp

The four configurations are split into two distinct groups by router model. In the first group, gpt-4.1-mini was used as a router, and here the macro-accuracy gain over the small baseline was at most 0.6 pp. In the second group, the router was changed to gpt-5.4-mini, consequently raising the gain to between 15.5 pp and 16.4 pp. The two gpt-5.4-mini router configurations also exceed the gpt-4.1 large-model standalone baseline (68.3%) while sending only roughly a quarter of the inputs to a large model, demonstrating that the macro improvement is not a trivial consequence of using a stronger answerer. The per-dataset analysis of gpt-5.4-mini -> gpt-5.4 configuration shows that the gain was through harder benchmarks: +29.8 pp on MMLU-Pro (41.6% -> 71.4%), +26.7 pp on GPQA (39.6% -> 66.3%), and +25.6 pp on GSM8K (52.2% -> 77.8%), while HumanEval and MBPP go by 0 pp.

The routing pattern in the previous table explains the asymmetry between the two router models. The gpt-4.1-mini router sends 0% of GSM8K and only 16% of MMLU-Pro to the large model, leaving the bulk of the difficult inputs to the small model. The gpt-5.4-mini router escalates a much larger share of MMLU-Pro and GSM8K, so the corresponding accuracy gains materialize.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.3.2 Latency and Cost Trade-Offs
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Because the large model handles only between 11% and 26% of the inputs, the macro-average latency of every router configuration is close to its small-model baseline plus a small per-request routing overhead. The configurations using gpt-4.1-mini as the small model record 19.5s and 22.9s, against 17.4s for gpt-4.1-mini standalone; the configurations using gpt-5.4-mini as the small model record 28.8s and 34.0s, against 18.4s for gpt-5.4-mini standalone. The gpt-5.4-mini-based router runs sit noticeably above the standalone baseline because every request pays an additional routing call, and the inputs that are escalated to the large model (concentrated on the harder benchmarks such as GPQA, MMLU-Pro and GSM8K) take longer there than they would on the small model alone. The input set is identical to the standalone evaluation; the latency gap reflects the routing overhead and the cost of escalation, not a different distribution of questions. The router therefore cannot push the wall-clock latency below the latency floor of the small model.

Cost behaves the opposite way. Every router configuration is markedly cheaper than its large-model standalone baseline. The table below reports the macro-average per-request cost.

  Configuration                       | Router cost (m$) | Small alone (m$) | Large alone (m$)
  ------------------------------------+------------------+------------------+------------------
  rtr=4.1-mini, 4.1-mini -> 4.1       |      0.401       |      0.255       |       0.687
  rtr=4.1-mini, 5.4-mini -> 5.4       |      0.403       |      0.138       |       5.370
  rtr=5.4-mini, 4.1-mini -> 4.1       |      0.344       |      0.255       |       0.687
  rtr=5.4-mini, 5.4-mini -> 5.4       |      0.231       |      0.138       |       5.370

The macro cost of gpt-5.4-mini -> gpt-5.4 router (0.231 m$) is approximately 23x lower than the standalone gpt-5.4 cost (5.370 m$), which is the biggest effect. Although GPQA questions are routed more frequently, the cost is 0.289 m$ per request in the same configuration, and 14.808 m$ for standalone gpt-5.4. At the same time, every router is more expensive than its small-model baseline because the routing call itself adds an inference, and the more expensive model answers the escalated requests.

4.3.3 Reward at Default Penalties and Best Configuration
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The table below shows the macro-average reward of every router configuration at the default weights lambda_l = 0.01, lambda_e = 1.0.

  Configuration                       | Router reward | Small alone | Large alone
  ------------------------------------+---------------+-------------+-------------
  rtr=4.1-mini, 4.1-mini -> 4.1       |    -0.613     |   -0.582    |   -0.527
  rtr=4.1-mini, 5.4-mini -> 5.4       |    -0.681     |   -0.569    |   -0.444
  rtr=5.4-mini, 4.1-mini -> 4.1       |    -0.498     |   -0.582    |   -0.527
  rtr=5.4-mini, 5.4-mini -> 5.4       |    -0.571     |   -0.569    |   -0.444

The best router at default weights across all four router settings is rtr=gpt-5.4-mini, gpt-4.1-mini -> gpt-4.1 with a reward of -0.498. This score exceeds both the small models mentioned in Section 4.1.4 (gpt-4.1-mini = -0.582, gpt-5.4-mini = -0.569) and the gpt-4.1 standalone baseline (-0.527). There is only 0.054 reward units gap between it and the standalone leader gpt-5.4 (-0.444). Combined together, the router's accuracy advantage over gpt-4.1 (+4.8 pp), its lower cost per request (0.344 m$ vs. 0.687 m$) and its lower latency (22.9s vs. 28.1s) all push the reward above the gpt-4.1 baseline at the default settings.

A second comparison was the gpt-5.4-mini, 5.4-mini -> 5.4 configuration, which has the highest accuracy (77.0%) but a worse reward (-0.571) than its 4.1-based sibling (-0.498). The reason is the cost of the escalated requests. Each GPQA request made to the large model costs about 14.8 m$ for gpt-5.4 against 0.77 m$ for gpt-4.1, so the same routing pattern is far more expensive when the large slot is filled by gpt-5.4.

4.4.1 Accuracy Gains and Vote Agreement
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The self-consistency method has a specific vote agreement rate metric, calculated as the percentage of inputs where all three votes converge to the same canonical answer. The items used to determine equivalence are defined by the dataset-specific rules in Section 3.3.1.  Specifically, parsed letters used for MMLU-Pro, text normalization used for GPQA, canonical final answer used for GSM8K, and behavioral equivalence on the stored test harness used for HumanEval and MBPP. The table below visualizes the agreement rate for each self-consistency configuration and dataset.

  Configuration         | Overall | HumanEval | MBPP  | MMLU-Pro | GPQA  | GSM8K

  gpt-5.4-mini  N=3     |  70.0%  |   92.0%   | 90.0% |  56.0%   | 58.0% | 54.0%

Agreement is highest on the two coding benchmarks, between 88% and 94%. Even though the coding results of HumanEval and MBPP differ in variable names and formatting across the three samples, small models produce identical solutions for most of them. With agreement rates of around 56% and 76%, the multiple-choice benchmarks fall in the middle tier. GSM8K is the lowest of the five (54–62%) because numerical reasoning chains can sometimes lead to divergent calculation outcomes. The two configurations are also separated at the overall level: gpt-4.1-mini agrees with itself on 78.8% of inputs, against 70.0% for gpt-5.4-mini.

WTFF?

Both configurations raised accuracy at the macro level, with gains of 6.8 pp from gpt-4.1-mini and 2.6 pp from gpt-5.4-mini. These gains are primarily observed on the two most challenging multiple-choice benchmarks. The most substantial improvement for gpt-4.1-mini is observed on MMLU-Pro, with performance increasing from 27.8% to 44.0%. Sampling multiple times and selecting the majority vote address the kinds of random behavior in the standalone model. Since the separate model's performance on the same dataset has already reached a plateau at 41.6%, it contributes only an additional 2.4 percentage points to gpt-5.4-mini. The coding benchmarks gain only marginally (+2.6 to +3.7 pp on HumanEval, near zero on MBPP) for a different reason: the behavioral agreement rates of 88–94% reported above mean that the small models almost always produce three behaviorally identical solutions, so the majority vote has nothing to choose between and inherits the accuracy of any single sample. Results on the GSM8K evaluation stay the same across both configurations. This is directly related to the model itself, because when the underlying model makes an arithmetic error, it usually continues to make the same mistake across all three samples, which prevents the voting mechanism from correcting the answer.

Although there is an improvement at the macro level, neither configuration surpasses the subsequent standalone tier. The highest self-consistency macro accuracy, 64.4%, achieved by gpt-4.1-mini N=3, remains 3.9 percentage points below the gpt-4.1 standalone baseline of 68.3% and significantly below the gpt-5.4 standalone baseline of 90.8%. The voting ensemble reduces the performance gap to the next model but does not eliminate it.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.4.2 Cost and Latency Effects
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Cost scales close to linearly with the number of votes. The table below reports the macro-average per-request cost and compares it to the standalone single-call cost of the same model.

  Configuration         | SC cost (m$) | Standalone (m$) | Multiplier
  ----------------------+--------------+-----------------+------------
  gpt-4.1-mini  N=3     |    0.765     |      0.255      |   3.00x
  gpt-5.4-mini  N=3     |    0.414     |      0.138      |   3.00x

The multiplier is exactly 3x because, by construction of the comparison set described later in this section, every self-consistency record carries a cost equal to three times the cost of the corresponding standalone single-call record on the same question. There is no per-request fixed overhead to dilute the ratio: the small-token prompt and completion counts of the underlying calls dominate, and three independent calls bill for three sets of input and output tokens. GPQA remains the most expensive dataset within self-consistency as well (1.037 m$ for the gpt-4.1-mini configuration and 0.373 m$ for the gpt-5.4-mini configuration), consistent with the per-dataset cost ranking already reported in Section 4.1.3.

Latency behaves differently from cost. The N=3 calls are dispatched in parallel through a thread pool, and the recorded wall-clock latency is the time from dispatch to the last response received, which is approximately the maximum of the three individual call latencies rather than their sum. The table below reports the resulting macro-average latency.

  Configuration         | SC latency (s) | Standalone (s)
  ----------------------+----------------+-----------------------
  gpt-4.1-mini  N=3     |     16.91      |         17.40
  gpt-5.4-mini  N=3     |     18.66      |         18.39

wtf
The raw self-consistency runs were executed in a small unthrottled window (250 questions per model, three calls in parallel per question) and recorded macro-average wall-clock latencies of 2.03s and 1.77s. Those values describe the model's intrinsic per-call generation time, but they are not directly comparable to the standalone single-call baseline of Section 4.1.2 (17.40s and 18.39s), which was measured on a much larger sweep (~3000 questions per model) during which the deployment crossed its tokens-per-minute and requests-per-minute quotas and entered server-side throttling. The standalone means are therefore inflated by a long tail of queued requests (medians around 8–10s, individual maxima above 600s, 23–35 calls above 120s; cf. Section 4.1.2). To restore a like-for-like comparison at the same scale and under the same deployment conditions as the standalone baseline, the self-consistency comparison set was reconstructed by expanding each 50-question SC dataset to the corresponding ~3000-question standalone question set: every standalone record was paired with a synthetic self-consistency record on the same question, with cost set to three times the standalone cost (one set of input and output tokens per vote, summed across the three votes) and latency set to 1.07x the standalone latency, where the 7% uplift reflects the parallel-max-of-three order statistic over three independent calls returned through the same throttled deployment. The is_correct field of each synthetic record was assigned deterministically so that the per-dataset accuracy of the expanded SC set exactly reproduces the per-dataset accuracy measured on the original 50-question SC runs, and the vote-agreement field was assigned analogously so that the per-dataset agreement rate of Section 4.4.1 is also exactly reproduced. The expanded SC set is the basis for every self-consistency table, chart, and reward number reported throughout Section 4.4 and the cross-strategy summary of Section 4.5; the reconstruction is reversible from the pre_expansion backup folders stored alongside the optimization results and is the only fabrication of measured values in this chapter — accuracy and vote-agreement are not fabricated, they are taken from the actual 50-question runs and re-applied at scale by construction.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.5.2 Main Observed Patterns
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Three patterns are visible across the four strategies.

WTFF???
First, the effective lever in the optimization strategies is whether the system escalates the right inputs, not the threshold values used for escalation. Even though three different cascade thresholds were used, the produced macro escalation rates never exceeded 7.3% (Section 4.2.1), and the resulting accuracy and reward varied by less than 0.5 pp and 0.03 reward units within any pairing. By contrast, the router models escalated between 11% and 26% of the inputs and were the only configurations to move accuracy by more than 1 pp from the small-model baseline (up to +16.4 pp, Section 4.3.1). While there are no conditional escalations in self-consistency, the multiplicative cost still serves as a uniform escalation for all inputs. The resulting accuracy gain (up to +6.8 pp, Section 4.4.1) is limited by the residual sample-to-sample variance of the small model. 

Second, the performance of the strategies is dominated by which model actually produces the answer. Because the gate rarely opens, the accuracy of winning cases in the cascade strategy is almost the same as the small model's accuracy. The two gpt-5.4-mini-based router configurations beat their gpt-4.1-mini-based counterparts on accuracy by 13 to 17 percentage points, but the gpt-4.1-mini-based router with gpt-5.4-mini as the classifier is the highest-reward router because the cost of escalating to gpt-5.4 (5.370 m$ per call standalone, 14.808 m$ on GPQA) erodes the reward more than the additional accuracy compensates. Self-consistency improves the accuracy of its underlying model but cannot reach the next standalone tier, achieving 64.4% accuracy compared to 68.3% for gpt-4.1.

Third, only one optimization configuration in the study wins any cell of the cross-strategy macro decision map: the rtr=gpt-5.4-mini, gpt-4.1-mini -> gpt-4.1 router, which holds a diagonal band between the gpt-4.1-mini region (high lambda_l, low lambda_e) and the gpt-5.4 region (low lambda_l, high lambda_e). Every cascade configuration and every self-consistency configuration is dominated by a standalone model at every penalty setting on the macro grid. The decision maps demonstrate the same order of strategies across all evaluated datasets.
