\chapter{Results}
\label{chapterResults}

This chapter reports the empirical observations of the study. All numbers, tables, and figures presented here follow the measurement and aggregation rules defined in Chapter~\ref{chapterMethod}: accuracy, latency, cost and economic reward are computed per record and then macro-averaged across the five benchmark datasets, so that each dataset contributes equally regardless of its sample count (cf.\ Section~\ref{sec:correctness} and Equation~\ref{eq:reward}). The interpretation of these results, the answers to the research questions, and the connection to prior literature are deferred to Chapter~\ref{chapterDiscussion}; this chapter restricts itself to what was measured.

% =============================================================================
\section{Standalone Model Results}
\label{sec:standalone-results}

The standalone evaluation establishes the reference point for all subsequent optimisation analyses. Each of the six Azure-hosted models introduced in Section~\ref{sec:studied-models} was evaluated on every benchmark from Section~\ref{sec:benchmark-tasks} using the prompts and decoding settings of Section~\ref{sec:datasets-prompts}. No routing, escalation, or resampling logic was applied: every input was answered by exactly one direct API call.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Accuracy and Dataset-Level Performance}
\label{sec:standalone-accuracy}

Figure~\ref{fig:standalone-accuracy} reports the macro-average accuracy obtained by each model across the five datasets, and Figure~\ref{fig:standalone-accuracy-per-ds} shows the same measurement broken down per dataset.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{charts/chart1_accuracy.png}
    \caption{Macro-average accuracy per standalone model, averaged across the five benchmark datasets with equal per-dataset weight. [chart1\_accuracy.png]}
    \label{fig:standalone-accuracy}
\end{figure}

The macro-average ranking partitions the six models into two clearly separated tiers. The reasoning-enabled models gpt-5.4-pro (93.2\%), gpt-5.4 (90.8\%) and o3-mini (83.3\%) all stay above 80\%, while gpt-4.1 (68.3\%), gpt-5.4-mini (60.6\%) and gpt-4.1-mini (57.6\%) cluster between 57\% and 69\%. The gap between the lowest top-tier model (o3-mini, 83.3\%) and the highest second-tier model (gpt-4.1, 68.3\%) is roughly 15 percentage points, larger than any gap inside either tier.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{charts/chart2_accuracy_per_dataset.png}
    \caption{Per-dataset accuracy of each standalone model. Bars are grouped by dataset; one bar per model within each group. [chart2\_accuracy\_per\_dataset.png]}
    \label{fig:standalone-accuracy-per-ds}
\end{figure}

The per-dataset breakdown in Figure~\ref{fig:standalone-accuracy-per-ds} shows that the macro-average ranking is not uniform across tasks. On HumanEval and MBPP all six models score between 81\% and 100\%; even the weakest model in the macro ranking, gpt-4.1-mini, reaches 91.4\% on HumanEval and 82.7\% on MBPP. The two coding benchmarks therefore offer little discriminating power for this set of models. The picture inverts on GPQA: the spread is the widest of any dataset (34.3\% for gpt-4.1-mini up to 84.0\% for gpt-5.4), and o3-mini, despite being a dedicated reasoning model, reaches only 52.8\% — below the non-reasoning gpt-5.4 (84.0\%) and gpt-5.4-pro (82.0\%). MMLU-Pro shows a sharp generation gap: the 4.1-generation pair stays at 50.1\% and 27.8\%, while the 5.4-generation pair reaches 92.0\% and 41.6\%. GSM8K shows a bimodal pattern in which the second-tier models cluster around 52\% and the top-tier models stay above 92\%.

A noteworthy outlier is gpt-4.1, which combines coding-specialist behaviour (92.4\% on HumanEval and 100.0\% on MBPP) with markedly lower accuracy on the knowledge- and reasoning-heavy benchmarks (50.1\% on MMLU-Pro, 45.7\% on GPQA, 53.1\% on GSM8K). Its macro-average of 68.3\% therefore hides a strong asymmetry across task families.

\subsection{Latency Results and Observed API Effects}
\label{sec:standalone-latency}

Wall-clock latency was measured per request as defined in Section~\ref{sec:latency-tokens}. Table~\ref{tab:standalone-latency} summarises both mean and median per-request latency, together with the number of requests that exceeded 120 seconds and the maximum value observed during the standalone runs.

\begin{table}[h]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
        \toprule
        Model        & Mean (s) & Median (s) & \# outliers $>120$\,s & Max (s) \\
        \midrule
        gpt-4.1      & 10.1  & 10.1 & 0   & 30.6 \\
        gpt-5.4      & 11.9  & 5.2  & 6   & 211.6 \\
        gpt-4.1-mini & 23.9  & 6.5  & 89  & 648.9 \\
        gpt-5.4-mini & 36.1  & 7.3  & 133 & 630.9 \\
        gpt-5.4-pro  & 47.2  & 22.3 & 18  & 888.8 \\
        o3-mini      & 61.6  & 16.6 & 107 & 760.0 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Wall-clock latency per request for each standalone model, including mean, median, count of requests above 120\,s and the maximum observed value.}
    \label{tab:standalone-latency}
\end{table}

The mean ranking in Table~\ref{tab:standalone-latency} is counter-intuitive: the small models are slower on average than their larger siblings (gpt-4.1-mini 23.9\,s vs.\ gpt-4.1 10.1\,s; gpt-5.4-mini 36.1\,s vs.\ gpt-5.4 11.9\,s). This pattern is not a property of the models themselves but of the deployment environment, as anticipated in the validity discussion of Section~\ref{sec:validity}. The medians invert the order — gpt-5.4 (5.2\,s), gpt-4.1-mini (6.5\,s) and gpt-5.4-mini (7.3\,s) are all faster than gpt-4.1 (10.1\,s) — and the rate-limit footprint of the small models is large: 89 and 133 requests above 120\,s for gpt-4.1-mini and gpt-5.4-mini respectively, against zero for gpt-4.1. This is consistent with the Azure documentation cited in Section~\ref{sec:cloud-deployment}, which states that latency degrades and 429 (Too Many Requests) responses appear once sustained traffic exceeds the assigned quota tier.

A smaller, genuine effect is also present. The mini models emit substantially more completion tokens than their larger siblings on the multiple-choice and short-answer benchmarks: on MMLU-Pro, gpt-4.1 produces an average of 9 completion tokens per request, while gpt-4.1-mini produces 150. Even on un-throttled requests this token-length difference contributes a modest latency increase. The combined picture is that the reported mean latencies should be read as deployment-level observations under realistic Azure conditions, not as intrinsic model speeds.

The two reasoning-enabled models exhibit the longest tails. o3-mini reaches a mean of 61.6\,s, dominated by MMLU-Pro (104.1\,s per request); gpt-5.4-pro reaches 47.2\,s, dominated by GPQA (91.2\,s per request). Both behaviours are consistent with the architectural property described in Section~\ref{sec:reasoning-inference}: extended internal deliberation increases generation time on the harder benchmarks while remaining moderate on simpler ones such as GSM8K (18.7\,s for o3-mini, 11.1\,s for gpt-5.4-pro).

\label{sec:standalone-cost}

The cost spread covers nearly three orders of magnitude, from gpt-5.4-mini at 0.138\,m\$ per request to gpt-5.4-pro at 106.021\,m\$ per request, a factor of approximately 769$\times$. Within the second tier, gpt-5.4-mini is even cheaper than gpt-4.1-mini despite belonging to a newer generation.

\section{Cascade Strategy Results}
\label{sec:cascade-results}

The cascade strategy specified in Section~\ref{sec:cascade-strategy} routes every input first through a small model, which emits both an answer and a self-reported confidence value, and forwards the input to the large model only when the confidence falls below the threshold $T$. Twelve configurations were evaluated: four small/large pairings combined with three thresholds $T \in \{60, 75, 90\}$.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Escalation Behavior and Accuracy}
\label{sec:cascade-accuracy}

The single most important measurement of the cascade experiment is the escalation rate, since the value of the strategy depends entirely on how often the large model is actually invoked. Table~\ref{tab:cascade-escalation} reports the macro-average escalation rate for each configuration.

\begin{table}[h]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{lrrr}
        \toprule
        Configuration & $T=60$ & $T=75$ & $T=90$ \\
        \midrule
        gpt-4.1-mini $\to$ gpt-4.1 & 0.1\% & 0.4\% & 1.1\% \\
        gpt-4.1-mini $\to$ gpt-5.4 & 0.0\% & 0.1\% & 0.1\% \\
        gpt-5.4-mini $\to$ gpt-4.1 & 0.1\% & 1.7\% & 7.3\% \\
        gpt-5.4-mini $\to$ gpt-5.4 & 0.0\% & 0.2\% & 1.0\% \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Macro-average escalation rate (percentage of inputs forwarded to the large model) for the twelve cascade configurations.}
    \label{tab:cascade-escalation}
\end{table}

Even at the most aggressive threshold $T=90$, the highest escalation rate observed is 7.3\% (gpt-5.4-mini $\to$ gpt-4.1), and the median value across the twelve configurations is below 1\%. At $T=60$ and $T=75$ escalation is essentially absent for most pairings. The combined overview in Figure~\ref{fig:cascade-overview} plots escalation rate alongside the resulting per-dataset accuracy for every configuration and confirms the same pattern at the dataset level: in most cells the small model alone produces the final answer.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{charts/chartC1_cascade_combined_overview.png}
    \caption{Per-dataset escalation rate and resulting cascade accuracy for every cascade configuration. [chartC1\_cascade\_combined\_overview.png]}
    \label{fig:cascade-overview}
\end{figure}

The mechanical consequence is that the threshold parameter $T$ has almost no effect on cascade accuracy. For every pairing, the macro-average accuracy varies by at most 0.5\,pp across the three thresholds. Table~\ref{tab:cascade-accuracy} reports the values together with the small and large standalone baselines for reference.

\begin{table}[h]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{lrrrrr}
        \toprule
        Configuration & $T=60$ & $T=75$ & $T=90$ & Small alone & Large alone \\
        \midrule
        gpt-4.1-mini $\to$ gpt-4.1 & 57.6\% & 57.7\% & 57.7\% & 57.6\% & 68.3\% \\
        gpt-4.1-mini $\to$ gpt-5.4 & 57.6\% & 57.6\% & 57.6\% & 57.6\% & 90.8\% \\
        gpt-5.4-mini $\to$ gpt-4.1 & 60.6\% & 60.6\% & 60.7\% & 60.6\% & 68.3\% \\
        gpt-5.4-mini $\to$ gpt-5.4 & 60.6\% & 60.7\% & 61.1\% & 60.6\% & 90.8\% \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Macro-average accuracy of each cascade configuration at the three thresholds, with the corresponding small-only and large-only standalone baselines. All cascade values are within 0.5\,pp of the small baseline.}
    \label{tab:cascade-accuracy}
\end{table}

The largest visible improvement is in the gpt-5.4-mini $\to$ gpt-5.4 pairing at $T=90$: macro-accuracy rises from 60.6\% (small alone) to 61.1\%, a gain of 0.5\,pp obtained with a 1.0\% escalation rate. At the dataset level, this cell is concentrated on GPQA (39.6\% $\to$ 40.7\%) and MMLU-Pro (41.6\% $\to$ 42.5\%), which is consistent with the harder benchmarks producing slightly more low-confidence outputs. In the gpt-5.4-mini $\to$ gpt-4.1 pairing, however, the escalated questions are forwarded to a model whose own GPQA accuracy (45.7\%) is only marginally higher than that of gpt-5.4-mini (39.6\%), so the small accuracy gain on the escalated subset does not propagate into a clear macro improvement.

The reason the threshold barely affects the result is that the modern small models used here rarely produce confidence values below 0.6, 0.75 or even 0.9. The escalation gate therefore almost never opens at any of the three thresholds tested, regardless of whether the underlying answer is correct. The cascade pipeline collapses, in practice, to running the small model alone for the overwhelming majority of inputs.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Latency and Cost Trade-Offs}
\label{sec:cascade-cost}

Because escalation is rare, the latency of every cascade configuration is dominated by the latency of its small model. The macro-average latency of the gpt-4.1-mini-based cascades stays close to 24\,s and that of the gpt-5.4-mini-based cascades close to 36\,s, mirroring the standalone numbers in Table~\ref{tab:standalone-latency}. The large-model component contributes only the small additional time required to handle the few escalated requests.

The cost picture is qualitatively similar but not identical. Table~\ref{tab:cascade-cost} compares the macro-average cost per request between $T=60$ and $T=90$ for each pairing.

\begin{table}[h]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{lrrr}
        \toprule
        Configuration & $T=60$ cost (m\$) & $T=90$ cost (m\$) & Small alone (m\$) \\
        \midrule
        gpt-4.1-mini $\to$ gpt-4.1 & 0.250 & 0.256 & 0.255 \\
        gpt-4.1-mini $\to$ gpt-5.4 & 0.250 & 0.278 & 0.255 \\
        gpt-5.4-mini $\to$ gpt-4.1 & 0.110 & 0.162 & 0.138 \\
        gpt-5.4-mini $\to$ gpt-5.4 & 0.111 & 0.251 & 0.138 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Macro-average per-request cost of each cascade configuration at $T=60$ and $T=90$, with the corresponding small-model standalone baseline.}
    \label{tab:cascade-cost}
\end{table}

At $T=60$ the cost is essentially that of the small model. At $T=90$, however, the gpt-5.4-mini $\to$ gpt-5.4 pairing rises from 0.111\,m\$ to 0.251\,m\$ — a 126\% increase driven entirely by the 1.0\% of inputs that hit the expensive large model on hard datasets. The same pairing on GPQA alone moves from 0.124\,m\$ at $T=60$ to 0.748\,m\$ at $T=90$, a sixfold increase produced by a 3.0\% escalation rate. This effect reflects the cost asymmetry already documented in Table~\ref{tab:standalone-cost}: a single GPQA call to gpt-5.4 costs about 14.8\,m\$, so a small number of escalations dominates the macro-average.

Figure~\ref{fig:cascade-dual-heatmap} summarises both dimensions in a single view: the left panel shows reward per cascade configuration and the right panel shows the corresponding average cost per request.

\section{Router Strategy Results}
\label{sec:router-results}

The router strategy specified in Section~\ref{sec:router-strategy} replaces the post-hoc confidence gate of the cascade with an upfront classifier model that, for each input, decides whether the small or the large model should produce the answer. Four configurations were evaluated, crossing two router models (gpt-4.1-mini, gpt-5.4-mini) with two small/large pairings.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Routing Behavior and Accuracy}
\label{sec:router-accuracy}

In contrast to the cascade, the router actually directs a substantial fraction of inputs to the large model. Table~\ref{tab:router-routing} reports the per-dataset routing rate to the large model for every configuration.

\begin{table}[h]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{lrrrrrr}
        \toprule
        Configuration & Overall & HumanEval & MBPP & MMLU-Pro & GPQA & GSM8K \\
        \midrule
        rtr=4.1-mini, 4.1-mini$\to$4.1   & 11.4\% & 1.8\% & 1.4\% & 16.0\% & 40.8\% & 0.0\% \\
        rtr=4.1-mini, 5.4-mini$\to$5.4   & 10.7\% & 1.2\% & 1.1\% & 15.3\% & 39.8\% & 0.0\% \\
        rtr=5.4-mini, 4.1-mini$\to$4.1   & 25.1\% & 0.6\% & 1.6\% & 36.8\% & 42.2\% & 21.4\% \\
        rtr=5.4-mini, 5.4-mini$\to$5.4   & 26.3\% & 0.6\% & 0.6\% & 36.6\% & 43.3\% & 25.8\% \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Fraction of inputs routed to the large model, overall and per dataset, for each router configuration.}
    \label{tab:router-routing}
\end{table}

The routing pattern is task-aware. All four configurations send less than 2\% of HumanEval and MBPP inputs to the large model; both code benchmarks are already handled well by the small model, as Section~\ref{sec:standalone-accuracy} showed. The bulk of the escalation is concentrated on GPQA (40--43\% across all configurations) and MMLU-Pro (16--37\%). GSM8K reveals a sharp difference between the two router models: the gpt-4.1-mini router treats GSM8K as easy (0\% escalated) while the gpt-5.4-mini router escalates 21--26\% of GSM8K inputs. Figure~\ref{fig:router-overview} visualises both the routing split and the resulting per-dataset accuracy for each configuration.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{charts/chartR1_router_overview.png}
    \caption{Per-dataset routing split (fraction of inputs sent to the large model) and resulting accuracy for each router configuration. [chartR1\_router\_overview.png]}
    \label{fig:router-overview}
\end{figure}

The accuracy outcomes follow directly from the routing behaviour. Table~\ref{tab:router-accuracy} reports the macro-average accuracy of every configuration alongside the gain over the corresponding small-model baseline.

\begin{table}[h]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
        \toprule
        Configuration & Router acc. & Small alone & Large alone & $\Delta$ vs.\ small \\
        \midrule
        rtr=4.1-mini, 4.1-mini$\to$4.1 & 58.2\% & 57.6\% & 68.3\% & $+0.6$\,pp \\
        rtr=4.1-mini, 5.4-mini$\to$5.4 & 60.8\% & 60.6\% & 90.8\% & $+0.2$\,pp \\
        rtr=5.4-mini, 4.1-mini$\to$4.1 & 73.1\% & 57.6\% & 68.3\% & $+15.5$\,pp \\
        rtr=5.4-mini, 5.4-mini$\to$5.4 & 77.0\% & 60.6\% & 90.8\% & $+16.4$\,pp \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Macro-average accuracy of each router configuration with corresponding small-only and large-only baselines.}
    \label{tab:router-accuracy}
\end{table}

The four configurations split into two distinct groups by router model. With gpt-4.1-mini as the router, the macro-accuracy gain over the small baseline is at most 0.6\,pp. With gpt-5.4-mini as the router, the gain rises to between 15.5\,pp and 16.4\,pp. The two gpt-5.4-mini router configurations also exceed the gpt-4.1 large-model standalone baseline (68.3\%) while sending only roughly a quarter of the inputs to a large model, demonstrating that the macro improvement is not a trivial consequence of using a stronger answerer. The per-dataset breakdown for the gpt-5.4-mini $\to$ gpt-5.4 router shows the gain concentrated exactly on the harder benchmarks: $+29.8$\,pp on MMLU-Pro (41.6\% $\to$ 71.4\%), $+27$\,pp on GPQA (39.6\% $\to$ 66.3\%) and $+26$\,pp on GSM8K (52.2\% $\to$ 77.8\%), while HumanEval and MBPP move by 0\,pp.

The asymmetry between the two router models is explained by the routing pattern of Table~\ref{tab:router-routing}. The gpt-4.1-mini router sends 0\% of GSM8K and only 16\% of MMLU-Pro to the large model, leaving the bulk of the difficult inputs in the hands of the small model. The gpt-5.4-mini router escalates a much larger share of MMLU-Pro and GSM8K, so the corresponding accuracy gains materialise.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Latency and Cost Trade-Offs}
\label{sec:router-cost}

Because the large model handles only between 11\% and 26\% of the inputs, the macro-average latency of every router configuration stays close to its small-model baseline plus a small per-request routing overhead of approximately 0.7--1.6\,s. The configurations using gpt-4.1-mini as the small model record 23.6\,s and 24.8\,s, against 23.9\,s for gpt-4.1-mini standalone; the configurations using gpt-5.4-mini as the small model record 36.9\,s and 37.7\,s, against 36.1\,s for gpt-5.4-mini standalone. The router cannot push the wall-clock latency below the latency floor of the small model.

Cost behaves the opposite way. Every router configuration is markedly cheaper than its large-model standalone baseline. Table~\ref{tab:router-cost} reports the macro-average per-request cost.

\begin{table}[h]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{lrrr}
        \toprule
        Configuration & Router cost (m\$) & Small alone (m\$) & Large alone (m\$) \\
        \midrule
        rtr=4.1-mini, 4.1-mini$\to$4.1 & 0.401 & 0.255 & 0.687 \\
        rtr=4.1-mini, 5.4-mini$\to$5.4 & 0.403 & 0.138 & 5.370 \\
        rtr=5.4-mini, 4.1-mini$\to$4.1 & 0.344 & 0.255 & 0.687 \\
        rtr=5.4-mini, 5.4-mini$\to$5.4 & 0.231 & 0.138 & 5.370 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Macro-average per-request cost of each router configuration with corresponding small-only and large-only baselines.}
    \label{tab:router-cost}
\end{table}

The most extreme case is the gpt-5.4-mini $\to$ gpt-5.4 router, whose macro cost (0.231\,m\$) is approximately $23\times$ lower than the gpt-5.4 standalone cost (5.370\,m\$). Even on the dataset that drives most of the routing, GPQA, the same configuration spends 0.289\,m\$ per request against 14.808\,m\$ for gpt-5.4 standalone. At the same time, every router is more expensive than its small-model baseline because the routing call itself adds an inference, and the escalated requests are answered by the more expensive model.

Figure~\ref{fig:router-dual-heatmap} reports both dimensions side by side: reward per router configuration on the left, average cost per request on the right.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{charts/chartR2_router_dual_heatmap.png}
    \caption{Side-by-side heatmaps of macro-average reward (left) and average cost per request (right) for the four router configurations and the corresponding standalone models. [chartR2\_router\_dual\_heatmap.png]}
    \label{fig:router-dual-heatmap}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Reward at Default Penalties and Best Configuration}
\label{sec:router-reward}

Table~\ref{tab:router-reward} reports the macro-average reward of every router configuration at the default weights $\lambda_{l}=0.01$, $\lambda_{e}=1.0$.

\begin{table}[h]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{lrrr}
        \toprule
        Configuration & Router reward & Small alone & Large alone \\
        \midrule
        rtr=4.1-mini, 4.1-mini$\to$4.1 & $-0.654$ & $-0.663$ & $-0.419$ \\
        rtr=4.1-mini, 5.4-mini$\to$5.4 & $-0.762$ & $-0.756$ & $-0.217$ \\
        rtr=5.4-mini, 4.1-mini$\to$4.1 & $-0.518$ & $-0.663$ & $-0.419$ \\
        rtr=5.4-mini, 5.4-mini$\to$5.4 & $-0.607$ & $-0.756$ & $-0.217$ \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Macro-average economic reward of each router configuration at default weights $\lambda_{l}=0.01$, $\lambda_{e}=1.0$, with the small-only and large-only baselines.}
    \label{tab:router-reward}
\end{table}

The best router at default weights is gpt-5.4-mini, gpt-4.1-mini $\to$ gpt-4.1 with a reward of $-0.518$. This value improves on both small-model baselines reported in Table~\ref{tab:standalone-reward} (gpt-4.1-mini $-0.663$, gpt-5.4-mini $-0.756$) and is the highest reward among the four router configurations. It does not, however, reach the gpt-4.1 standalone baseline ($-0.419$) or the overall standalone leader gpt-5.4 ($-0.217$). The router's accuracy advantage over gpt-4.1 ($+4.8$\,pp) and its lower per-request cost (0.344\,m\$ vs.\ 0.687\,m\$) are outweighed at the default weights by the additional latency: 24.8\,s for the router versus 10.1\,s for gpt-4.1 standalone, contributing about $0.15$ extra reward units of latency penalty per request.

A second comparison within the router group is also visible in Table~\ref{tab:router-reward}: the gpt-5.4-mini, 5.4-mini$\to$5.4 configuration has the highest accuracy (77.0\%) but a worse reward ($-0.607$) than its 4.1-based sibling ($-0.518$). The reason is the cost of the escalated requests: each large-model GPQA call costs about 14.8\,m\$ for gpt-5.4 against 0.77\,m\$ for gpt-4.1, so the same routing pattern is far more expensive when the large slot is filled by gpt-5.4.
