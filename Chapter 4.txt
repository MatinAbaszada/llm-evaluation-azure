Chapter 4. Results

This chapter reports the empirical observations of the study. All numbers, tables, and figures presented here follow the measurement and aggregation rules defined in Chapter 3: accuracy, latency, cost, and economic reward are computed per record and then macro-averaged across the five benchmark datasets, so that each dataset contributes equally regardless of its sample count (cf. Section 3.3.1 and the reward equation in Section 3.3.4). The interpretation of these results, the answers to the research questions, and the connection to prior literature are deferred to Chapter 5; this chapter restricts itself to what was measured.


================================================================================
4.1 Standalone Model Results
================================================================================

The standalone evaluation establishes the reference point for all subsequent optimization analyses. Each of the six Azure-hosted models introduced in Section 3.2.1 was evaluated on every benchmark from Section 3.2.2 using the prompts and decoding settings of Section 3.2.3. No routing, escalation, or resampling logic was applied: every input was answered by exactly one direct API call.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.1.1 Accuracy and Dataset-Level Performance
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The macro-average accuracy of each standalone model, taken with equal weight across the five datasets, is reported in the figure below.

[chart1_accuracy.png]

The macro-average ranking partitions the six models into two clearly separated tiers. The reasoning-enabled models gpt-5.4-pro (93.2%), gpt-5.4 (90.8%) and o3-mini (83.3%) all stay above 80%, while gpt-4.1 (68.3%), gpt-5.4-mini (60.6%) and gpt-4.1-mini (57.6%) cluster between 57% and 69%. The gap between the lowest top-tier model (o3-mini, 83.3%) and the highest second-tier model (gpt-4.1, 68.3%) is roughly 15 percentage points, larger than any gap inside either tier.

[chart2_accuracy_per_dataset.png]

The per-dataset breakdown shows that the macro-average ranking is not uniform across tasks. On HumanEval and MBPP all six models score between 81% and 100%; even the weakest model in the macro ranking, gpt-4.1-mini, reaches 91.4% on HumanEval and 82.7% on MBPP. The two coding benchmarks therefore offer little discriminating power for this set of models. The picture inverts on GPQA: the spread is the widest of any dataset (34.3% for gpt-4.1-mini up to 84.0% for gpt-5.4), and o3-mini, despite being a dedicated reasoning model, reaches only 52.8% — below the non-reasoning gpt-5.4 (84.0%) and gpt-5.4-pro (82.0%). MMLU-Pro shows a sharp generation gap: the 4.1-generation pair stays at 50.1% and 27.8%, while the 5.4-generation pair reaches 92.0% and 41.6%. GSM8K shows a bimodal pattern in which the second-tier models cluster around 52% and the top-tier models stay above 92%.

A noteworthy outlier is gpt-4.1, which combines coding-specialist behaviour (92.4% on HumanEval and 100.0% on MBPP) with markedly lower accuracy on the knowledge- and reasoning-heavy benchmarks (50.1% on MMLU-Pro, 45.7% on GPQA, 53.1% on GSM8K). Its macro-average of 68.3% therefore hides a strong asymmetry across task families.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.1.2 Latency Results and Observed API Effects
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Wall-clock latency was measured per request as defined in Section 3.3.2. The table below summarises both mean and median per-request latency, together with the number of requests that exceeded 120 seconds and the maximum value observed during the standalone runs.

  Model         | Mean (s) | Median (s) | # outliers >120s | Max (s)
  --------------+----------+------------+------------------+---------
  gpt-4.1-mini  |   15.8   |     9.6    |       35         |  634.7
  gpt-5.4-mini  |   17.4   |     8.2    |       23         |  630.9
  gpt-4.1       |   20.9   |     6.0    |       54         |  648.9
  gpt-5.4       |   34.7   |     6.1    |       10         |  607.5
  gpt-5.4-pro   |   54.3   |    17.9    |       32         |  888.8
  o3-mini       |   61.6   |    15.4    |      107         |  760.0

The mean ranking follows the conventional pattern: within each generation the small model is faster on average than its larger sibling (gpt-4.1-mini 15.8s vs. gpt-4.1 20.9s; gpt-5.4-mini 17.4s vs. gpt-5.4 34.7s). The medians, however, paint a different picture: gpt-4.1 (6.0s) and gpt-5.4 (6.1s) are below their respective minis (9.6s and 8.2s). The combination of a low median with a higher mean indicates that the bulk of large-model requests complete quickly but a non-trivial number of long-running outliers shift the average upward; the same effect, more pronounced, drives the means of the minis themselves. The number of requests above 120s sits in the same broad range across the deployments (10–54 for the four general-purpose models, 32 for gpt-5.4-pro and 107 for o3-mini), a footprint consistent with the Azure documentation cited in Section 2.5: latency degrades and 429 (Too Many Requests) responses appear once sustained traffic exceeds the assigned quota tier, and the reasoning-enabled deployments also contribute long internal-deliberation tails on top of the throughput effect.

A smaller, genuine effect is also present. The mini models emit substantially more completion tokens than their larger siblings on the multiple-choice and short-answer benchmarks: on MMLU-Pro, gpt-4.1 produces an average of 9 completion tokens per request, while gpt-4.1-mini produces 150. Even on un-throttled requests this token-length difference contributes a modest latency increase. The combined picture is that the reported mean latencies should be read as deployment-level observations under realistic Azure conditions, not as intrinsic model speeds.

The two reasoning-enabled models exhibit the longest tails. o3-mini reaches a mean of 61.6s, dominated by MMLU-Pro (104.1s per request); gpt-5.4-pro reaches 54.3s, dominated by GPQA (95.0s per request). Both behaviours are consistent with the architectural property described in Section 2.3: extended internal deliberation increases generation time on the harder benchmarks while remaining moderate on simpler ones such as GSM8K (18.7s for o3-mini, 11.1s for gpt-5.4-pro).

4.1.3 Token Usage and Cost Results
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Per-request cost was computed deterministically from token counts using the cost equation of Section 3.3.3 and the Azure OpenAI list prices recorded there. The table below reports the macro-average cost per request and the cost on GPQA, which turns out to be the most expensive dataset for every model. Values are reported in milli-dollars (1 m$ = 0.001 USD).

  Model         | Macro avg cost/req (m$) | GPQA cost/req (m$)
  --------------+-------------------------+--------------------
  gpt-5.4-mini  |          0.138          |        0.124
  gpt-4.1-mini  |          0.255          |        0.346
  gpt-4.1       |          0.687          |        0.775
  o3-mini       |          4.659          |        8.805
  gpt-5.4       |          5.370          |       14.808
  gpt-5.4-pro   |        106.021          |      295.182

The cost spread covers nearly three orders of magnitude, from gpt-5.4-mini at 0.138 m$ per request to gpt-5.4-pro at 106.021 m$ per request, a factor of approximately 769x. GPQA is consistently the most expensive dataset across all models because of its long, multi-part scientific prompts and the longer reasoning traces they elicit; the GPQA cost reaches 295.182 m$ per request for gpt-5.4-pro and 14.808 m$ for gpt-5.4. Within the second tier, gpt-5.4-mini is even cheaper than gpt-4.1-mini despite belonging to a newer generation.

[chart3_cost_vs_accuracy.png]

The cost–accuracy plot exposes which models lie on the empirical Pareto frontier. gpt-5.4-mini occupies the cheapest corner; gpt-5.4 dominates the upper-middle of the plane by combining 90.8% accuracy with a cost roughly 20x below gpt-5.4-pro; gpt-5.4-pro provides the maximum accuracy but at a cost that places it far to the right of every other point. o3-mini and gpt-4.1 are not Pareto-dominant under this projection: each is matched or exceeded on either accuracy or cost by another model.

================================================================================
4.2 Cascade Strategy Results
================================================================================

The cascade strategy specified in Section 3.4.3 routes every input first through a small model, which emits both an answer and a self-reported confidence value, and forwards the input to the large model only when the confidence falls below the threshold T. Twelve configurations were evaluated: four small/large pairings combined with three thresholds T in {60, 75, 90}.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.2.1 Escalation Behavior and Accuracy
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The single most important measurement of the cascade experiment is the escalation rate, since the value of the strategy depends entirely on how often the large model is actually invoked. The table below reports the macro-average escalation rate for each configuration.

  Configuration                  | T=60 | T=75 | T=90
  -------------------------------+------+------+------
  gpt-4.1-mini -> gpt-4.1        | 0.1% | 0.4% | 1.1%
  gpt-4.1-mini -> gpt-5.4        | 0.0% | 0.1% | 0.1%
  gpt-5.4-mini -> gpt-4.1        | 0.1% | 1.7% | 7.3%
  gpt-5.4-mini -> gpt-5.4        | 0.0% | 0.2% | 1.0%

Even at the most aggressive threshold T=90, the highest escalation rate observed is 7.3% (gpt-5.4-mini -> gpt-4.1), and the median value across the twelve configurations is below 1%. At T=60 and T=75 escalation is essentially absent for most pairings. The combined overview below plots escalation rate alongside the resulting per-dataset accuracy for every configuration and confirms the same pattern at the dataset level: in most cells the small model alone produces the final answer.

[chartC1_cascade_combined_overview.png]

The mechanical consequence is that the threshold parameter T has almost no effect on cascade accuracy. For every pairing, the macro-average accuracy varies by at most 0.5 pp across the three thresholds. The table below reports the values together with the small and large standalone baselines for reference.

  Configuration                  | T=60  | T=75  | T=90  | Small alone | Large alone
  -------------------------------+-------+-------+-------+-------------+-------------
  gpt-4.1-mini -> gpt-4.1        | 57.6% | 57.7% | 57.7% |    57.6%    |    68.3%
  gpt-4.1-mini -> gpt-5.4        | 57.6% | 57.6% | 57.6% |    57.6%    |    90.8%
  gpt-5.4-mini -> gpt-4.1        | 60.6% | 60.6% | 60.7% |    60.6%    |    68.3%
  gpt-5.4-mini -> gpt-5.4        | 60.6% | 60.7% | 61.1% |    60.6%    |    90.8%

The largest visible improvement is in the gpt-5.4-mini -> gpt-5.4 pairing at T=90: macro-accuracy rises from 60.6% (small alone) to 61.1%, a gain of 0.5 pp obtained with a 1.0% escalation rate. At the dataset level, this cell is concentrated on GPQA (39.6% -> 40.7%) and MMLU-Pro (41.6% -> 42.5%), which is consistent with the harder benchmarks producing slightly more low-confidence outputs. In the gpt-5.4-mini -> gpt-4.1 pairing, however, the escalated questions are forwarded to a model whose own GPQA accuracy (45.7%) is only marginally higher than that of gpt-5.4-mini (39.6%), so the small accuracy gain on the escalated subset does not propagate into a clear macro improvement.

The reason the threshold barely affects the result is that the modern small models used here rarely produce confidence values below 0.6, 0.75 or even 0.9. The escalation gate therefore almost never opens at any of the three thresholds tested, regardless of whether the underlying answer is correct. The cascade pipeline collapses, in practice, to running the small model alone for the overwhelming majority of inputs.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.2.2 Latency and Cost Trade-Offs
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Because escalation is rare, the latency of every cascade configuration is dominated by the latency of its small model. The macro-average latency of the gpt-4.1-mini-based cascades stays around 17.7–18.3s and that of the gpt-5.4-mini-based cascades around 28.5–31.3s. These figures sit somewhat above the standalone numbers reported in Section 4.1.2 (15.8s and 17.4s respectively), reflecting both the small fraction of escalated requests and the longer-running mix of throttled inputs that fell into the cascade runs. The large-model component contributes only the additional time required to handle the few escalated requests.

The cost picture is qualitatively similar but not identical. The table below compares the macro-average cost per request between T=60 and T=90 for each pairing.

  Configuration                  | T=60 cost (m$) | T=90 cost (m$) | Small alone (m$)
  -------------------------------+----------------+----------------+------------------
  gpt-4.1-mini -> gpt-4.1        |     0.250      |     0.256      |       0.255
  gpt-4.1-mini -> gpt-5.4        |     0.250      |     0.278      |       0.255
  gpt-5.4-mini -> gpt-4.1        |     0.110      |     0.162      |       0.138
  gpt-5.4-mini -> gpt-5.4        |     0.111      |     0.251      |       0.138

At T=60 the cost is essentially that of the small model. At T=90, however, the gpt-5.4-mini -> gpt-5.4 pairing rises from 0.111 m$ to 0.251 m$ — a 126% increase driven entirely by the 1.0% of inputs that hit the expensive large model on hard datasets. The same pairing on GPQA alone moves from 0.124 m$ at T=60 to 0.748 m$ at T=90, a sixfold increase produced by a 3.0% escalation rate. This effect reflects the cost asymmetry already documented in Section 4.1.3: a single GPQA call to gpt-5.4 costs about 14.8 m$, so a small number of escalations dominates the macro-average.

================================================================================
4.3 Router Strategy Results
================================================================================

The router strategy specified in Section 3.4.2 replaces the post-hoc confidence gate of the cascade with an upfront classifier model that, for each input, decides whether the small or the large model should produce the answer. Four configurations were evaluated, crossing two router models (gpt-4.1-mini, gpt-5.4-mini) with two small/large pairings.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.3.1 Routing Behavior and Accuracy
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

In contrast to the cascade, the router actually directs a substantial fraction of inputs to the large model. The table below reports the per-dataset routing rate to the large model for every configuration.

  Configuration                       | Overall | HumanEval | MBPP  | MMLU-Pro | GPQA  | GSM8K
  ------------------------------------+---------+-----------+-------+----------+-------+-------
  rtr=4.1-mini, 4.1-mini -> 4.1       |  11.4%  |   1.8%    | 1.4%  |  16.0%   | 40.8% |  0.0%
  rtr=4.1-mini, 5.4-mini -> 5.4       |  10.7%  |   1.2%    | 1.1%  |  15.3%   | 39.8% |  0.0%
  rtr=5.4-mini, 4.1-mini -> 4.1       |  25.1%  |   0.6%    | 1.6%  |  36.8%   | 42.2% | 21.4%
  rtr=5.4-mini, 5.4-mini -> 5.4       |  26.3%  |   0.6%    | 0.6%  |  36.6%   | 43.3% | 25.8%

The routing pattern is task-aware. All four configurations send less than 2% of HumanEval and MBPP inputs to the large model; both code benchmarks are already handled well by the small model, as Section 4.1.1 showed. The bulk of the escalation is concentrated on GPQA (40–43% across all configurations) and MMLU-Pro (16–37%). GSM8K reveals a sharp difference between the two router models: the gpt-4.1-mini router treats GSM8K as easy (0% escalated) while the gpt-5.4-mini router escalates 21–26% of GSM8K inputs. The figure below visualises both the routing split and the resulting per-dataset accuracy for each configuration.

[chartR1_router_overview.png]

The accuracy outcomes follow directly from the routing behaviour. The table below reports the macro-average accuracy of every configuration alongside the gain over the corresponding small-model baseline.

  Configuration                       | Router acc | Small alone | Large alone | Delta vs small
  ------------------------------------+------------+-------------+-------------+----------------
  rtr=4.1-mini, 4.1-mini -> 4.1       |   58.2%    |    57.6%    |    68.3%    |    +0.6 pp
  rtr=4.1-mini, 5.4-mini -> 5.4       |   60.8%    |    60.6%    |    90.8%    |    +0.2 pp
  rtr=5.4-mini, 4.1-mini -> 4.1       |   73.1%    |    57.6%    |    68.3%    |   +15.5 pp
  rtr=5.4-mini, 5.4-mini -> 5.4       |   77.0%    |    60.6%    |    90.8%    |   +16.4 pp

The four configurations split into two distinct groups by router model. With gpt-4.1-mini as the router, the macro-accuracy gain over the small baseline is at most 0.6 pp. With gpt-5.4-mini as the router, the gain rises to between 15.5 pp and 16.4 pp. The two gpt-5.4-mini router configurations also exceed the gpt-4.1 large-model standalone baseline (68.3%) while sending only roughly a quarter of the inputs to a large model, demonstrating that the macro improvement is not a trivial consequence of using a stronger answerer. The per-dataset breakdown for the gpt-5.4-mini -> gpt-5.4 router shows the gain concentrated exactly on the harder benchmarks: +29.8 pp on MMLU-Pro (41.6% -> 71.4%), +27 pp on GPQA (39.6% -> 66.3%) and +26 pp on GSM8K (52.2% -> 77.8%), while HumanEval and MBPP move by 0 pp.

The asymmetry between the two router models is explained by the routing pattern in the previous table. The gpt-4.1-mini router sends 0% of GSM8K and only 16% of MMLU-Pro to the large model, leaving the bulk of the difficult inputs in the hands of the small model. The gpt-5.4-mini router escalates a much larger share of MMLU-Pro and GSM8K, so the corresponding accuracy gains materialise.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.3.2 Latency and Cost Trade-Offs
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Because the large model handles only between 11% and 26% of the inputs, the macro-average latency of every router configuration is close to its small-model baseline plus a small per-request routing overhead. The configurations using gpt-4.1-mini as the small model record 19.5s and 22.9s, against 15.8s for gpt-4.1-mini standalone; the configurations using gpt-5.4-mini as the small model record 28.8s and 34.0s, against 17.4s for gpt-5.4-mini standalone. The gpt-5.4-mini-based router runs sit noticeably above the standalone baseline because they were dominated by harder, slower inputs and by the additional time consumed by the escalated requests. The router cannot push the wall-clock latency below the latency floor of the small model.

Cost behaves the opposite way. Every router configuration is markedly cheaper than its large-model standalone baseline. The table below reports the macro-average per-request cost.

  Configuration                       | Router cost (m$) | Small alone (m$) | Large alone (m$)
  ------------------------------------+------------------+------------------+------------------
  rtr=4.1-mini, 4.1-mini -> 4.1       |      0.401       |      0.255       |       0.687
  rtr=4.1-mini, 5.4-mini -> 5.4       |      0.403       |      0.138       |       5.370
  rtr=5.4-mini, 4.1-mini -> 4.1       |      0.344       |      0.255       |       0.687
  rtr=5.4-mini, 5.4-mini -> 5.4       |      0.231       |      0.138       |       5.370

The most extreme case is the gpt-5.4-mini -> gpt-5.4 router, whose macro cost (0.231 m$) is approximately 23x lower than the gpt-5.4 standalone cost (5.370 m$). Even on the dataset that drives most of the routing, GPQA, the same configuration spends 0.289 m$ per request against 14.808 m$ for gpt-5.4 standalone. At the same time, every router is more expensive than its small-model baseline because the routing call itself adds an inference, and the escalated requests are answered by the more expensive model.

4.3.3 Reward at Default Penalties and Best Configuration
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The table below reports the macro-average reward of every router configuration at the default weights lambda_l = 0.01, lambda_e = 1.0.

  Configuration                       | Router reward | Small alone | Large alone
  ------------------------------------+---------------+-------------+-------------
  rtr=4.1-mini, 4.1-mini -> 4.1       |    -0.613     |   -0.582    |   -0.527
  rtr=4.1-mini, 5.4-mini -> 5.4       |    -0.681     |   -0.569    |   -0.444
  rtr=5.4-mini, 4.1-mini -> 4.1       |    -0.498     |   -0.582    |   -0.527
  rtr=5.4-mini, 5.4-mini -> 5.4       |    -0.571     |   -0.569    |   -0.444

The best router at default weights is rtr=gpt-5.4-mini, gpt-4.1-mini -> gpt-4.1 with a reward of -0.498. This value improves on both small-model baselines reported in Section 4.1.4 (gpt-4.1-mini -0.582, gpt-5.4-mini -0.569) and on the gpt-4.1 standalone baseline (-0.527), and is the highest reward among the four router configurations. It does not reach the overall standalone leader gpt-5.4 (-0.444), but the gap is only 0.054 reward units. The router's accuracy advantage over gpt-4.1 (+4.8 pp) and its lower per-request cost (0.344 m$ vs. 0.687 m$) jointly outweigh its higher latency (22.9s vs. 20.9s) at the default weights.

A second comparison within the router group is also visible in the table above: the gpt-5.4-mini, 5.4-mini -> 5.4 configuration has the highest accuracy (77.0%) but a worse reward (-0.571) than its 4.1-based sibling (-0.498). The reason is the cost of the escalated requests: each large-model GPQA call costs about 14.8 m$ for gpt-5.4 against 0.77 m$ for gpt-4.1, so the same routing pattern is far more expensive when the large slot is filled by gpt-5.4.
