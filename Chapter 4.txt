\chapter{Results} \label{ch:results}

This chapter reports the empirical observations of the study. All numbers, tables, and figures in this chapter follow the measurement and aggregation rules defined in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}. Accuracy, latency, cost, and economic reward are both computed per record and then macro-averaged across the five benchmark datasets, in order to ensure that each dataset contributes equally regardless of its sample count (cf.\ Section~\ref{subsec:correctness} and the reward equation in Equation~\ref{eq:reward}).

\section{Standalone Model Results} \label{sec:standalone}

The standalone evaluation establishes the reference point for all subsequent optimization analyses. Each of the six Azure-hosted models introduced in Section~\ref{subsec:studied_models} was evaluated on every benchmark from Section~\ref{subsec:benchmark_tasks} using the prompts and decoding settings of Section~\ref{subsec:datasets_prompts}. To get the answer for each question, exactly one API call was made, without any routing, escalation, or resampling.

Figure~\ref{fig:chart1_accuracy} shows the macro-average score of each individual model, where each of the five datasets carries equal weight.

Based on macro-average ranking, the models are separated into two groups. The first group consists of reasoning models, namely gpt-5.4-pro (93.2\%), gpt-5.4 (90.8\%), and o3-mini (83.3\%), and all of them surpass the 80\% threshold. On the other hand, the second group includes gpt-4.1 (68.3\%), gpt-5.4-mini (60.6\%), and gpt-4.1-mini (57.6\%), which sit between 57\% and 69\%. o3-mini sets the lower bound of the top tier at 83.3\%, and gpt-4.1 defines the upper bound of the second tier at 68.3\%. The gap between them is roughly 15 percent, exceeding any gap within either tier.

At the level of individual datasets, the macro-average rank is not consistent for the tasks. On HumanEval and MBPP all six models score between 81\% and 100\%. Even the weakest model in the macro ranking, gpt-4.1-mini, reaches 91.4\% on HumanEval and 82.7\% on MBPP. The two coding benchmarks therefore offer little discriminating power for this set of models. The picture inverts on GPQA: the spread is the widest of any dataset (34.3\% for gpt-4.1-mini up to 84.0\% for gpt-5.4), and o3-mini, despite being a dedicated reasoning model, reaches only 52.8\%. The result of o3-mini is even below the non-reasoning gpt-5.4 (84.0\%) and gpt-5.4-pro (82.0\%). Regarding the MMLU-Pro, the 4.1-generation pair stays at 50.1\% and 27.8\%, while the 5.4-generation pair reaches 90.0\% and 41.6\%. It is possible to see a bimodal distribution in the GSM8K dataset because the low level models are clustered around 52\%, whereas the high level models exceed 92\%.

Between the models, gpt-4.1 is a noteworthy outlier. It achieved 92.4\% average accuracy on the HumanEval tasks and 100.0\% on the MBPP tasks, indicating good performance on coding tasks. However, on knowledge and reasoning benchmarks, the model scores just 50.1\% on MMLU-Pro, 45.7\% on GPQA, and 53.1\% on GSM8K. Even though the performance is distributed unevenly, the macro average of the model hides this pattern.

\subsection{Latency Results and Observed API Effects} \label{subsec:standalone_latency}

Wall-clock latency was measured per request as defined in Section~\ref{subsec:latency_token}. Table~\ref{tab:standalone_latency} summarises both mean and median per-request latency along with the number of requests that exceeded 120 seconds and the maximum value observed during the standalone runs.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Per-request latency statistics for the six standalone models, aggregated across all benchmark records.}
\label{tab:standalone_latency}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
Model & Mean (s) & Median (s) & \# outliers $>$120s & Max (s) \\
\midrule
gpt-4.1-mini & 17.4 & 9.6 & 35 & 634.7 \\
gpt-5.4-mini & 18.4 & 8.2 & 23 & 630.9 \\
gpt-4.1 & 28.1 & 6.0 & 54 & 648.9 \\
gpt-5.4 & 34.7 & 6.1 & 10 & 607.5 \\
gpt-5.4-pro & 53.1 & 17.8 & 32 & 888.8 \\
o3-mini & 65.7 & 15.4 & 107 & 760.0 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Conventional pattern could be observed for the mean ranking. On average, the small model is faster than its larger counterpart (gpt-4.1-mini 17.4s vs.\ gpt-4.1 28.1s; gpt-5.4-mini 18.4s vs.\ gpt-5.4 34.7s). The reverse is true for the medians, where gpt-4.1 (6.0s) and gpt-5.4 (6.1s) are below their respective minis (9.6s and 8.2s). The low median with a higher mean indicates that the bulk of large-model requests complete quickly, but a small number of long-running outliers shift the average upward. The same effect drives the means of the minis themselves. Requests above 120s are roughly similar across all deployments (10 to 54 for the four general-purpose models, 32 for gpt-5.4-pro, and 107 for o3-mini). This matches with the description written in Section~\ref{sec:cloud_constraints}. As the quota tier is reached, deployment latency degrades, and this is usually the case for reasoning-enabled models because they perform additional internal processing.

A smaller, genuine effect is also present. The mini models emit substantially more completion tokens than their larger siblings on the multiple-choice and short-answer benchmarks. On MMLU-Pro, gpt-4.1 produces an average of 9 completion tokens per request, while gpt-4.1-mini produces 150. This variation in the number of tokens leads to a minor increase in latency even in unthrottled requests. Therefore, instead of pure model speeds, the reported mean latencies should be understood as deployment-level observations.

The two reasoning-enabled models exhibit the longest tails. Average of o3-mini was 65.7s, mostly due to MMLU-Pro (104.1s per request) and the same was 53.1s for gpt-5.4-pro, mostly due to GPQA (104.9s per request). Both behaviors are consistent with the architectural property discussed in Section~\ref{sec:reasoning_inference}, which states that for complex tasks, inference generation takes longer due to internal deliberation, while the generation time remains reasonable for simpler ones, such as GSM8K (18.7s for o3-mini, 14.1s for gpt-5.4-pro).

\section{Cascade Strategy Results} \label{sec:cascade_results}

The cascade strategy specified in Section~\ref{subsec:cascade_strategy} first routes each input through a small model, then forwards it to the large model if its confidence falls below the threshold $T$. A total of twelve configurations were evaluated. These include four small and large pairings combined with three thresholds $T \in \{60, 75, 90\}$.

\subsection{Escalation Behavior and Accuracy} \label{subsec:cascade_escalation}

The single most important measurement of the cascade experiment is the escalation rate, since the value of the strategy depends entirely on how often the large model is actually invoked. Table~\ref{tab:cascade_escalation} reports the macro-average escalation rate for each configuration.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Macro-average escalation rate (fraction of requests forwarded to the large model) for each cascade configuration.}
\label{tab:cascade_escalation}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
Configuration & $T=60$ & $T=75$ & $T=90$ \\
\midrule
gpt-4.1-mini $\rightarrow$ gpt-4.1 & 0.1\% & 0.4\% & 1.1\% \\
gpt-4.1-mini $\rightarrow$ gpt-5.4 & 0.0\% & 0.1\% & 0.1\% \\
gpt-5.4-mini $\rightarrow$ gpt-4.1 & 0.1\% & 1.7\% & 7.3\% \\
gpt-5.4-mini $\rightarrow$ gpt-5.4 & 0.0\% & 0.2\% & 1.0\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Even at the most aggressive threshold, where $T$ was set to 90, there was only 7.3\% escalation with the median escalation rate of the twelve experiments falling below 1\%. At $T=60$ and $T=75$, there was almost no escalation for most pairings. So, in most cases, the small model produced the final answer alone. Figure~\ref{fig:chartC1_cascade_combined_overview} plots the escalation rate and the achieved per dataset accuracy for each configuration, and it is obvious that the same pattern holds at the dataset level.

\begin{figure}[h]

\caption{Per-dataset escalation rate and accuracy for the twelve cascade configurations.}
\label{fig:chartC1_cascade_combined_overview}
\end{figure}

The mechanical consequence is that the threshold parameter $T$ has almost no effect on cascade accuracy. In all variants, the macro-average accuracy varies by at most 0.5 pp across all three thresholds. Table~\ref{tab:cascade_accuracy} reports the values, along with the small and large standalone baselines, for reference.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering

\label{tab:cascade_accuracy}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrr}
\toprule
Configuration & $T=60$ & $T=75$ & $T=90$ & Small alone & Large alone \\
\midrule
gpt-4.1-mini $\rightarrow$ gpt-4.1 & 57.7\% & 57.7\% & 57.7\% & 57.6\% & 68.3\% \\
gpt-4.1-mini $\rightarrow$ gpt-5.4 & 57.6\% & 57.7\% & 57.7\% & 57.6\% & 90.8\% \\
gpt-5.4-mini $\rightarrow$ gpt-4.1 & 60.6\% & 60.4\% & 60.5\% & 60.6\% & 68.3\% \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The largest visible increase is 0.5 pp, which can be seen when increasing the threshold to 90 for the gpt-5.4-mini $\rightarrow$ gpt-5.4 cell. This improvement is the result of increases in GPQA (39.6\% to 40.7\%) and MMLU-Pro (41.6\% to 42.5\%). This aligns with the observation that the model tends to return lower confidence on harder questions. In contrast, for the gpt-5.4-mini $\rightarrow$ gpt-4.1 cell, the questions escalated by threshold increase go to a model that only has a slightly higher GPQA accuracy than the gpt-5.4-mini (45.7\% vs.\ 39.6\%), so there is not a noticeable macro-accuracy improvement even though there was a slight accuracy gain in the cell for the escalated subset.

The reason for such a low escalation rate is that the modern small models are excessively confident and rarely produce confidence values below 0.6, 0.75, or even 0.9. Therefore, the escalation gate almost never opens at any of the three thresholds tested, regardless of whether the underlying answer is correct or wrong. This forces the cascade pipeline to run the small model alone for the overwhelming majority of inputs.

\subsection{Latency and Cost Trade-Offs} \label{subsec:cascade_tradeoffs}

Because escalation is rare, the latency of every cascade configuration is dominated by the latency of its small model. The macro-average latency of the gpt-4.1-mini-based cascades stays around 17.7--18.3s and that of the gpt-5.4-mini-based cascades around 28.5--31.3s. They are slightly higher than the standalone metrics shown in Section~\ref{subsec:standalone_latency} (17.4s and 18.4s, respectively) due to a small number of escalated requests that took longer to execute. The large-model component contributes only the additional time required to handle the few escalated requests.

The cost picture is qualitatively similar but not identical. Table~\ref{tab:cascade_cost} compares the macro-average cost per request between $T=60$ and $T=90$ for each pairing.

The cost asymmetry documented in Section~\ref{subsec:standalone_cost} reflects this effect. A single GPQA call to gpt-5.4 costs around 14.8 m\$, which leads to the macro-average being dominated by a small number of escalations.

\section{Router Strategy Results} \label{sec:router_results}

The router strategy specified in Section~\ref{subsec:router_strategy} replaces the post-hoc confidence gate of the cascade with an upfront classifier model that, for each input, decides whether the small or the large model should produce the answer. Four configurations were evaluated, crossing two router models (gpt-4.1-mini, gpt-5.4-mini) with two small/large pairings.

\subsection{Routing Behavior and Accuracy} \label{subsec:router_routing}

In contrast to the cascade, the router actually directs a substantial fraction of inputs to the large model. Table~\ref{tab:router_routing} reports the per-dataset routing rate to the large model for every configuration.

\begin{table}[h]

\subsection{Latency and Cost Trade-Offs} \label{subsec:router_tradeoffs}

Because the large model handles only between 11\% and 26\% of the inputs, the macro-average latency of every router configuration is close to its small-model baseline plus a small per-request routing overhead. The configurations using gpt-4.1-mini as the small model record 19.5s and 22.9s, against 17.4s for gpt-4.1-mini standalone; the configurations using gpt-5.4-mini as the small model record 28.8s and 34.0s, against 18.4s for gpt-5.4-mini standalone. The gpt-5.4-mini-based router runs sit noticeably above the standalone baseline because every request pays an additional routing call, and the inputs that are escalated to the large model (concentrated on the harder benchmarks such as GPQA, MMLU-Pro and GSM8K) take longer there than they would on the small model alone. The input set is identical to the standalone evaluation; the latency gap reflects the routing overhead and the cost of escalation, not a different distribution of questions. The router therefore cannot push the wall-clock latency below the latency floor of the small model.

Cost behaves the opposite way. Every router configuration is markedly cheaper than its large-model standalone baseline. Table~\ref{tab:router_cost} reports the macro-average per-request cost.

\section{Self-Consistency Results} \label{sec:sc_results}

The self-consistency strategy specified in Section~\ref{subsec:selfcons_strategy} was instantiated with $N=3$ for both gpt-4.1-mini and gpt-5.4-mini.

The most substantial improvement for gpt-4.1-mini is observed on MMLU-Pro, with performance increasing from 27.8\% to 44.0\%. The voting ensemble reduces the performance gap to the next model but does not eliminate it.

\subsection{Main Observed Patterns} \label{subsec:cross_patterns}

Three patterns are visible across the four strategies.

First, the effective lever in the optimization strategies is whether the system escalates the right inputs, not the threshold values used for escalation. Even though three different cascade thresholds were used, the produced macro escalation rates never exceeded 7.3\% (Section~\ref{subsec:cascade_escalation}), and the resulting accuracy and reward varied by less than 0.5 pp and 0.03 reward units within any pairing. By contrast, the router models escalated between 11\% and 26\% of the inputs and were the only configurations to move accuracy by more than 1 pp from the small-model baseline (up to $+$16.4 pp, Section~\ref{subsec:router_routing}). While there are no conditional escalations in self-consistency, the multiplicative cost still serves as a uniform escalation for all inputs. The resulting accuracy gain (up to $+$6.8 pp, Section~\ref{subsec:sc_accuracy}) is limited by the residual sample-to-sample variance of the small model.

Second, the performance of the strategies is dominated by which model actually produces the answer. Because the gate rarely opens, the accuracy of winning cases in the cascade strategy is almost the same as the small model's accuracy. The two gpt-5.4-mini-based router configurations beat their gpt-4.1-mini-based counterparts on accuracy by roughly 15 to 16 percentage points ($+$14.9 pp and $+$16.2 pp), but the rtr=gpt-5.4-mini, gpt-4.1-mini $\rightarrow$ gpt-4.1 configuration is the highest-reward router because the additional latency of escalating to gpt-5.4 (34.0s vs.\ 22.9s macro, costing about 0.111 reward units at $\lambda_{l} = 0.01$) outweighs the $+$3.9 pp accuracy gain (about 0.039 reward units at $\lambda_{e} = 1.0$). Cost works in the opposite direction at the macro level. The 5.4-based router is actually slightly cheaper per request (0.231 m\$ vs.\ 0.344 m\$) because only $\sim$25\% of inputs escalate to the expensive large model and the small-model floor (gpt-5.4-mini, 0.138 m\$) is itself below gpt-4.1-mini (0.255 m\$). Self-consistency improves the accuracy of its underlying model but cannot reach the next standalone tier, achieving 64.4\% accuracy compared to 68.3\% for gpt-4.1.

Third, two optimization configurations in the study win cells of their respective cross-strategy macro decision maps. The first one is rtr=gpt-5.4-mini, gpt-4.1-mini $\rightarrow$ gpt-4.1 router, which holds a diagonal band between the gpt-4.1-mini region (high $\lambda_{l}$, low $\lambda_{e}$) and the gpt-5.4 region (low $\lambda_{l}$, high $\lambda_{e}$) and the second one is the gpt-4.1-mini $N=3$ self-consistency configuration, which dominates the upper-left half of the SC-vs-standalone map. Every cascade configuration is dominated by a standalone model at every penalty setting on the macro grid. The per-dataset decision maps show that the ordering of strategies depends on the benchmark because the cascades and additional router configurations win cells on MMLU-Pro and GSM8K that they do not win on the macro average.
