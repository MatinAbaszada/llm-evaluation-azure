**RQ1.** How do selected Azure-hosted LLMs differ in output quality, latency, and cost across representative tasks?

**RQ2.** Under which conditions do reasoning-enabled models provide enough benefit to justify their additional cost and latency?

**RQ3.** To what extent can a proxy-based optimization layer improve the cost-efficiency of Azure-hosted LLM applications without unacceptable quality loss?


================================================================================
5. DISCUSSION
================================================================================

This chapter interprets the results of Chapter 4 and answers the three research questions. Numerical values are repeated only where needed to support a claim.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.1 Trade-Offs among Standalone Models
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The six standalone models do not lie on a single quality–cost continuum but form two clearly separated capability tiers. The reasoning-enabled top tier (gpt-5.4-pro 93.2%, gpt-5.4 90.8%, o3-mini 83.3%) all sit above 80% macro-accuracy, while gpt-4.1, gpt-5.4-mini and gpt-4.1-mini cluster between 57–68%. The ~15 pp gap between o3-mini (83.3%) and gpt-4.1 (68.3%) exceeds any gap within either tier and is best read as a generation/architecture step rather than a smooth scaling curve.

On the cost axis the spread is far larger than on the quality axis: per-request cost varies by ~770× (gpt-5.4-mini 0.138 m$ vs gpt-5.4-pro 106.0 m$), while macro-accuracy varies only by ~36 pp. As a result no model Pareto-dominates. gpt-5.4 is the closest to a global optimum: it pairs near-frontier accuracy (90.8%) with a moderate cost (5.37 m$) and the lowest median latency (6.1 s), which is why it wins the economic-reward ranking at the default weights (-0.444) despite not being the most accurate model. gpt-5.4-pro adds only +2.4 pp accuracy over gpt-5.4 at ~20× the cost and ~1.5× the mean latency, illustrating sharply diminishing returns at the frontier and dropping to fifth place in the reward ordering (-0.705).

The latency results require careful interpretation. The mean ranking is conventional (mini models faster than their flagships, e.g. gpt-4.1-mini 17.4 s vs gpt-4.1 28.1 s), but the medians reverse it: gpt-4.1 (6.0 s) and gpt-5.4 (6.1 s) sit below their respective minis (9.6 s and 8.2 s). The flagships' higher means despite lower medians indicate that the bulk of their requests complete quickly while a small number of long-running calls (10–54 outliers above 120 s for the four general-purpose models, 32 for gpt-5.4-pro and 107 for o3-mini) drag the average upward. This is not a model-quality finding but an operational one: under realistic Azure deployment conditions, observed latency is governed by quota tier and throttling at least as much as by model size — particularly for reasoning-enabled models, whose internal deliberation amplifies the tail. Reward-based decisions in production should therefore rely on percentile latencies rather than means.

The answer to RQ1 is therefore: the differences between Azure-hosted LLMs are large and multi-dimensional, dominated by cost variation and by a generational accuracy step rather than by a smooth quality/latency frontier; the "best" model is application-dependent and can only be identified once the relative weights of error, latency and cost are fixed.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.2 The Case for Reasoning-Enabled Inference
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Reasoning-enabled inference helps only on a narrow band of tasks and only under specific cost/latency regimes.

o3-mini, the dedicated reasoning model, beats all 4.1 models on procedural tasks (94.1% HumanEval, 94.0% GSM8K) but underperforms gpt-5.4 and gpt-5.4-pro on knowledge-heavy GPQA (52.8% vs 82–84%). Extended deliberation therefore pays off where the bottleneck is procedure (arithmetic chains, code), but not where it is stored knowledge (graduate-level science). This is consistent with the recent observation that reasoning under output-length pressure does not monotonically improve quality (Empirical Study of LLM Reasoning under Strict Output-Length Constraint).

Reasoning also imposes the heaviest latency tail in the study: o3-mini averages 65.7 s and reaches 104.1 s on MMLU-Pro, while gpt-5.4-pro averages 53.1 s and reaches 104.9 s on GPQA. Combined with its mid-range accuracy, o3-mini's tail drives it to the worst economic reward of all six models at the default penalties (-0.787) — even though it is the third most accurate. gpt-5.4-pro, the most accurate model, drops from first in accuracy to fifth in reward (-0.705) for the same reason: each additional accuracy point at the frontier costs disproportionately more dollars and seconds, and a ~100 m$ and ~20 s gap to gpt-5.4 is not recovered by a 2.4 pp accuracy gain at default weights.

Reasoning models therefore justify their additional cost and latency only when (i) the task is procedural and benefits from chain-of-thought, (ii) the application weights error far more heavily than latency or cost (the high-λ_error region in the heatmaps where gpt-5.4-pro wins cells), and (iii) the deployment can absorb tens of seconds per request. For latency-sensitive or high-throughput Azure workloads they are not the rational choice; the lighter gpt-5.4 dominates such regions in both the macro reward and the (λ_error, λ_latency) decision map.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.3 Effectiveness of Proxy-Based Optimization
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The proxy-style optimization layer (cascade, router, self-consistency) implemented in this study can improve cost-efficiency, but the three strategies differ sharply in how much, and only one of them defeats every standalone model on a substantial region of the (λ_error, λ_latency) decision map.

Cascade is largely neutralised by LLM overconfidence. Across all twelve configurations the small model escalates ≤ 1.1% of requests at T=60/75 and at most 7.3% even at T=90, because modern aligned LLMs rarely produce confidence values below 0.6, 0.75 or 0.9 — a calibration issue documented in the LLM evaluation literature (Systematic Survey on Evaluating LLMs). As a consequence every cascade behaves, in practice, like its small model with a small overhead: macro-accuracy varies by at most 0.5 pp across the three thresholds, the best cascade reward is -0.601 (gpt-4.1-mini → gpt-5.4 at T=75) which sits below both small standalones, and no cascade configuration wins any cell of the macro decision map. Cascades only emerge as winners in narrow per-dataset regions on MMLU-Pro and GSM8K (chart C6). The threshold hyper-parameter is effectively inert under current calibration.

Routing produces the best optimization result of the study. Hybrid-LLM-style upfront difficulty classification (Hybrid LLM: Cost-Efficient and Quality-Aware Query Routing; Cross-Attention Routing for Cost-Aware LLM Selection) succeeds where cascade fails because the routing decision is made by an external classifier rather than by the small model's own confidence. The gpt-5.4-mini router escalates ~25% of requests — concentrated on GPQA (~42%), MMLU-Pro (~37%) and GSM8K (~22–26%) — and lifts macro-accuracy by +15.5 pp and +16.4 pp over the small baseline, with the gpt-5.4-mini → gpt-5.4 configuration reaching 77.0%. The best reward of the entire study (-0.498) is achieved by the gpt-5.4-mini router with gpt-4.1-mini / gpt-4.1 as the worker pair: it improves on the gpt-4.1-mini standalone (-0.582), the gpt-5.4-mini standalone (-0.569) and even the gpt-4.1 standalone (-0.527), and it is the only optimization configuration in the study that wins a substantial region of the macro decision map (chart R3, the diagonal band between the gpt-4.1-mini and gpt-5.4 areas). Router quality dominates worker quality: replacing the gpt-4.1-mini router with the gpt-5.4-mini router raises macro-accuracy by ~14.9 pp for the same workers, primarily because gpt-5.4-mini correctly identifies hard MMLU-Pro and GSM8K items that gpt-4.1-mini does not.

Self-consistency improves reward modestly and wins the upper-left of the heatmap. Three-vote majority lifts gpt-4.1-mini macro-accuracy by +6.8 pp (57.6% → 64.4%) and gpt-5.4-mini by +2.6 pp (60.6% → 63.2%) at exactly 3× the per-request cost. Because the three calls are dispatched in parallel, latency rises by only ~1.07× rather than 3× (gpt-4.1-mini 17.4 → 18.6 s; gpt-5.4-mini 18.4 → 19.7 s), which is why the gpt-4.1-mini N=3 configuration improves reward to -0.543 and dominates the upper-left region of the macro decision map (chart SC3) where latency and cost are weighted lightly. Gains concentrate on the multiple-choice benchmarks (MMLU-Pro, GPQA) where vote variance is high and the model occasionally captures the correct option; on coding tasks the agreement rate is already 88–94%, leaving voting nothing to choose between, and on GSM8K the model tends to repeat the same arithmetic mistake across all three samples. Self-consistency cannot close the generational gap to gpt-4.1 standalone (64.4% vs 68.3%).

Why no proxy strategy displaces gpt-5.4 in the accuracy-sensitive region. gpt-5.4 standalone sits at an exceptionally favourable operating point (90.8% accuracy at 5.37 m$ and a 6.1 s median latency) and dominates any region of the heatmap where accuracy is weighted moderately or higher. Optimization helps when the deployment is constrained — forced to use a cheap small model as the worker, or operating under a fixed monthly budget — but in an unconstrained comparison the strongest standalone model remains hard to beat, consistent with the broader inference-serving literature (Taming the Titans).

The answer to RQ3 is: a proxy layer can meaningfully improve cost-efficiency when (a) the workload includes a mixture of easy and hard requests, (b) an external router with better calibration than the worker is available, and (c) the cost or latency budget rules out the strongest standalone model. Under these conditions a learned router beats every standalone model in part of the penalty space; without them, parallel self-consistency is a useful low-cost accuracy lift on small models, while confidence-based cascading is not viable on current Azure-hosted LLMs without an explicit calibration step.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.4 Relation to Prior Literature
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The standalone-model results align with the price/quality heterogeneity reported in the Economic Evaluation of LLMs work and confirm that headline accuracy is a poor single criterion for deployment.

The router results reproduce the central claim of Hybrid LLM and Cross-Attention Routing — that a learned upfront classifier can recover most of a frontier model's accuracy at a fraction of its cost — within the Azure OpenAI ecosystem and with current 5.4-class models. The study extends the prior work in two directions: first by showing that router model choice dominates worker pair choice for net accuracy, and second by integrating the routing comparison into a unified economic-reward framework that also penalises latency.

The cascade results are more negative than typically reported in early cascade literature, where reasonable escalation rates were assumed. The underlying cause — systematic overconfidence of modern aligned LLMs — is exactly the calibration failure highlighted in the Systematic Survey on Evaluating LLMs. This study contributes a concrete quantification of how that calibration failure neutralises confidence-gated cascading on five standard benchmarks.

The self-consistency results are consistent with the original SC observation (sub-linear accuracy gain with N) and with Taming the Titans' broader point that multi-sample inference is a cost amplifier whose value depends on whether parallel dispatch is available.

Prompt-compression techniques such as LLMLingua were not evaluated here, but the cost analysis suggests they would primarily help on the high-context / high-output GPQA and MMLU-Pro datasets where token usage dominates cost.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.5 Implications for Azure Deployment
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The results of this study translate into a small set of concrete deployment guidelines for Azure-based LLM applications. Model selection should be driven by the unified economic reward rather than by headline accuracy: in the evaluated landscape gpt-5.4 emerges as the default rational choice for general workloads, gpt-5.4-mini is the appropriate target whenever cost is the dominant constraint and roughly 60% accuracy is acceptable, and gpt-5.4-pro is justified only in low-throughput scenarios where accuracy strictly outweighs both latency and price.

Measured latency should be treated as a property of the Azure deployment rather than of the model itself. Quota tier, region and time-of-day throttling can dominate the per-query latency observed by an application, as the mean-versus-median analysis in Chapter 4 makes explicit. For this reason production decisions should rely on percentile latencies (P50 and P95) measured under the actual deployed quota, not on vendor-published or small-sample numbers.

Reasoning-enabled models should be used selectively. They are well suited to procedural tasks such as code generation and multi-step mathematics, and to accuracy-critical low-throughput pipelines, but they are a poor fit for chat-style or interactive workloads with strict latency budgets, where a flagship such as gpt-5.4 dominates.

When optimization is required, a learned router is preferable to confidence-gated cascading. Without an explicit calibration step, cascade thresholds are inert on current Azure-hosted LLMs, while a small router model with better calibration than the worker is the most effective strategy observed in this study. Self-consistency remains a useful but narrow tool: it provides a cheap accuracy lift only when the N calls can be dispatched in parallel, when the per-query latency budget is not the binding constraint, and when the worker is a small model whose error pattern is noisy rather than systematic.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.6 Contributions
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The central contribution of this thesis is a unified economic-reward framework that combines accuracy, latency and cost into a single scalar and exposes the trade-off explicitly through (λ_error, λ_latency) sweeps, so that deployment decisions can be grounded in a per-application weighting rather than in a single global metric. Within this framework the study provides an empirical comparison of six current Azure-hosted models spanning the 4.1 and 5.4 generations, the mini, full and pro tiers, and both the standard and reasoning paradigms, evaluated on five standard benchmarks under identical conditions.

A second contribution is the side-by-side evaluation of three proxy-based optimization strategies — cascade, router and self-consistency — within the same reward framework. The comparison shows that learned routing is the only strategy that meaningfully improves over the standalone baseline, and that confidence-based cascading is currently neutralised by the systematic overconfidence of modern aligned LLMs. Finally, the work contributes an explicit treatment of Azure-specific operational effects, in particular API throttling, quota-driven latency outliers, and the distinction between mean and median latency reporting; these effects are usually absent from academic LLM evaluations but directly govern real deployment outcomes.
